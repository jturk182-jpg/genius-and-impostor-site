The Unstoppable Reader

Can you ignore what you read? Let’s find out. Something appears on the screen, and all you have to do is tap the button that matches the color you see. Nothing else about it matters, just the color.

There are four short rounds, and the whole thing takes about two minutes. Go as fast as you can without guessing.

What it means

So what did this game just show you? A very advanced system in your brain, working in the background. It reads every word you see, instantly, with zero effort, and you are never aware of it running. It is so good at its job that you can’t switch it off, even when the game rewards switching it off.

Normally that system is invisible. Its work is effortless and non-conscious, so we think nothing of it. You only noticed it today because the game made its output collide with your goal for a moment, and the collision showed up as a few extra milliseconds. In real life that collision almost never happens: the word is exactly what you want to read, and reading it instantly is part of what makes you fluent. A game like this is just a way to catch the system in the act, and to appreciate something that is always running and usually transparent.

Why none of it feels impressive

And notice how strange it is that reading feels like nothing. Reading feels like nothing precisely because reading runs outside of consciousness. We only register the work we can feel, so the most sophisticated things our brains do are the things we never think about. This blind spot has a name in The Genius and the Impostor: the Effort Inversion. Michelangelo supposedly said that if people knew how hard he had worked for his mastery, it wouldn’t seem so wonderful at all. Your reading is the same kind of mastery: so well practiced that the effort has become invisible, even to you.

Sports make this blind spot easier to see, because in sports the training is visible. Nobody watches a beginner’s wobbly tennis swing and calls it more athletic than a champion’s smooth one. With mental skills, the skilled part runs invisibly, so the skilled part gets none of the credit. Your slowdown in round 3 came from being extremely good at reading.

Scratching the surface

And reading is just one of these systems. Right now your brain is also decoding speech the instant you hear it, recognizing every face you glance at, keeping your body balanced, and predicting the next words of every sentence you take in. A musician hears a chord and knows its flavor without trying. A touch typist’s fingers find the keys on their own. Everything you’ve mastered runs this way: built through practice, then handed off to machinery that runs by itself, outside of consciousness. This game made one of those systems visible for two minutes. There are many more running as you read this.

Where this story comes from

Everything in this game is one small piece of The Genius and the Impostor, a book about the two characters who share your head. The Genius is the vast non-conscious machinery that just read every word on this page for you. The Impostor is the conscious narrator, the part of you that was following the instructions and keeping score.

In a color game, the Impostor only costs you a few milliseconds. But when something real is on the line, the Impostor can get loud enough to drown out the Genius entirely. You’ve practiced, you’ve prepared, and the part of you that knows what to do goes quiet at exactly the wrong moment. The book is about why that happens, and how to make sure it doesn’t.
