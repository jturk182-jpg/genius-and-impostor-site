The story behind the game

Meet Ayumu

A young chimpanzee in Japan out-remembers university students at a number game, and everybody who repeats the story leaves out the best part.

The game

Numbers pop up in random spots on a touchscreen. A moment later they turn into blank tiles. Now touch the tiles in order, smallest number first, entirely from memory. One wrong touch and the round is over.

The thing the researchers changed was how long the numbers stayed visible before the blanks arrived. They tried 650 milliseconds, then 430, then 210. Two chimpanzees played, along with nine human university students.

What happened

The humans got worse as the flashes got shorter, which is exactly what everybody expected. So did Ai, the older chimpanzee. Ayumu, who was five at the time, did not. He stayed at roughly 80% correct all the way down, including at the shortest flash, where the students dropped to somewhere between 20 and 40 percent.

210 Milliseconds. Ayumu barely notices.

Those numbers come from secondary sources and from the Edinburgh replication paper. The original is a brief letter with no abstract, and the human figures at 650 and 430 milliseconds are not confirmed here, which is why the game itself only ever compares you at 210.

Now the part nobody mentions

Before that test, Ayumu had played this game about five hundred times.

The students had played it about fifty.

That gap in practice is not a footnote to the result. It is most of the result. When other researchers went back and gave people a proper amount of practice, the famous difference started closing in a hurry. Silberberg and Kearns found that well-practised humans matched Ayumu at five numbers. Cook and Wilson trained two university students until they were better than the chimpanzee.

The bit worth remembering500 against 50

The popular version of this story is about hardware: chimpanzees, it says, simply come with a better memory than we do. The practice counts tell a different story and a far more useful one. Two players were compared at a game where one had ten times the practice of the other, and the one with the practice won. That happens in every field anyone has ever measured, including yours.

A replication at Edinburgh found something more precise. People do better than the original letter suggested, and still come in under the chimpanzees, with the remaining difference sitting specifically in holding places in mind rather than in memory generally. Later work by Read and colleagues put chimpanzee working memory somewhere around that of a four or five year old child, well short of an adult's.

Why your eyes cannot save you

Here is a genuinely delightful physical fact. Your eyes travel across a scene in quick hops called saccades rather than gliding smoothly, and every hop takes time to plan and to make. A single hop from the middle of your vision out to the edge takes longer than 210 milliseconds all on its own. Bartz measured this back in 1962 and nobody has found a way around it since.

So at Ayumu's speed the numbers vanish before your eyes could possibly have visited them one by one, which makes looking for the 1 and then hunting for the 2 not just slow but impossible. Something else has to happen instead: the whole board has to arrive at once and be held. That is a different skill, and the cheerful news is that it can be learned, which is exactly what the training side of the game is for.

The chimpanzee who knew symbols did worse

Here is the quiet finding tucked inside the original study. Ai, the older chimpanzee, scored below Ayumu. Ai was the one who had been taught to use symbols years earlier.

Matsuzawa's suggestion is that all that symbol knowledge comes with a bill attached. Meaning gets attached to the numbers, and the attaching takes up room the task badly needs. The same pattern shows up in people in several other places, and it is one of the more interesting threads in this whole area: a great deal of skilled performance turns out to involve getting the part of you that interprets to be quiet long enough for the part that perceives to do its job.

How this game plays fair

The scored round is Ayumu's exact setting, nine numbers at 210 milliseconds, because an easier version would not be worth comparing to anything. Every single flash is timed against your screen's own refresh rather than simply assumed to be right, and any try where the timing wandered or the screen skipped a frame gets thrown out and replaced instead of quietly counted. You can see the whole log on your results.

Then you get the dials, and the practice Ayumu got and you did not. Your map fills in, your edge moves, and that moving edge is the actual point of all of this.
