The story behind the game

Meet Ayumu

A young chimpanzee in Japan out-remembers university students at a number game, and everybody who repeats the story leaves out the best part.

The game, and there are two of them

Numbers pop up in random spots on a touchscreen. A moment later they turn into blank tiles. Now touch the tiles in order, smallest number first, entirely from memory. One wrong touch and the round is over.

That much is common to both versions Inoue and Matsuzawa ran, and the difference between them is where nearly every retelling of this story goes wrong, including, until recently, ours.

The first version puts the clock on the numbers. Five numbers appear and stay for a fixed moment, 650 milliseconds, or 430, or 210, and then everything masks at once. You get exactly the time they give you. Two chimpanzees played this one, along with nine human university students.

The second version hands the clock to you. All nine numbers, one to nine, sit on the screen for as long as you like. The instant you touch the 1, the other eight vanish and you finish from memory. Nobody imposes a deadline; you set it yourself by deciding when you have looked long enough.

What happened

On the timed version, the humans got worse as the flashes got shorter, which is exactly what everybody expected. So did Ai, the older chimpanzee. Ayumu, who was five at the time, did not. He stayed at roughly 80% correct all the way down, including at the shortest flash, where the students dropped to somewhere between 20 and 40 percent.

On the self-paced version he did something different, and arguably stranger. All nine numbers, and he committed to that first touch in about two thirds of a second, then finished the sequence correctly more than eight times out of ten.

Those are two separate feats, and blurring them together invents a chimpanzee who never existed: one who does all nine in a fifth of a second. He does five in a fifth of a second, or nine in two thirds. Both are remarkable, and they are not the same trick.

210 Milliseconds. Ayumu barely notices.

Those numbers come from secondary sources and from a later restaging of the task. The original is a brief letter with no abstract, and the human figures at 650 and 430 milliseconds are not confirmed here, which is why the game itself only ever compares you at 210.

Now the part nobody mentions

Before that test, Ayumu had played this game about five hundred times.

The students had played it about fifty.

That gap in practice does most of the explaining here, all by itself. When other researchers went back and gave people a proper amount of practice, the famous difference started closing in a hurry. Silberberg and Kearns found that with training, humans reached Ayumu's level at five numbers. Cook and Wilson made the sharper point of all: the original test pitted a chimp who had practised the game for months against people seeing it for the very first time, which is not really a fair way to find out whose memory is better.

The bit worth remembering500 against 50

The popular version of this story is about hardware: chimpanzees, it says, simply come with a better memory than we do. The practice counts tell a different story and a far more useful one. Two players were compared at a game where one had ten times the practice of the other, and the one with the practice won. That happens in every field anyone has ever measured, including yours.

Why your eyes cannot save you

Here is a genuinely delightful physical fact. Your eyes travel across a scene in quick hops called saccades rather than gliding smoothly, and every hop takes time to plan and to make. A single hop from the middle of your vision out to the edge takes longer than 210 milliseconds all on its own. Bartz measured this back in 1962 and nobody has found a way around it since.

So at Ayumu's speed the numbers vanish before your eyes could possibly have visited them one by one, which makes looking for the 1 and then hunting for the 2 not just slow but impossible. Something else has to happen instead. The whole board has to arrive at once.

Hold on. Whose memory is bigger?

Here is a puzzle that ought to sink this entire story.

Read and colleagues went back through the evidence on this and put chimpanzee working memory somewhere in the range of a four or five year old child's, comfortably short of an adult human's. Yours is bigger. So how on earth does Ayumu beat university students?

Because this game never asked how much you can hold. It asked how much you can grab.

Think back to the eye problem. At 210 milliseconds you get exactly one look. Nothing gets scanned, nothing gets checked a second time. So the whole thing comes down to how much of that single glance you can capture and pin down before it fades. That is a question about intake, not about storage. What Ayumu does better is get more out of one look.

A later restaging of the task sharpened it further. Roberts and Quillinan put it online and collected some sixteen thousand rounds from volunteers, and they found that people do better than the original letter suggested while still coming in under the chimpanzees. The difference that remains is specifically about remembering where things were, rather than about memory in general. Their volunteers could pick out the first number about as often as Ayumu did. Holding on to where all of them sat is the part that fell apart.

The bit that makes it clickNot a memory contest

If Ayumu simply had a roomier memory, then "he has a roomier memory" would be the answer and there would be nothing else to say. He does not have one. Which means the answer has to be about what each mind does with a glance, and that is where this gets genuinely interesting.

The chimpanzee who knew symbols did worse

Here is the quiet finding tucked inside the original study. Ai, the older chimpanzee, scored below Ayumu. Ai was the one who had been taught to use symbols years earlier.

Matsuzawa's suggestion is that all that symbol knowledge comes with a bill attached. Meaning gets attached to the numbers, and the attaching takes up room the task badly needs.

And there is your answer to the puzzle. You cannot take a raw snapshot of that board, because on the way in you are already turning what you see into names and meanings. By the time a 7 reaches you, it has stopped being a shape in a place and turned into seven. That conversion is the most useful thing your mind does all day, and it is happening at exactly the moment the task needed you to just look. Ai, who had more of it than Ayumu, paid more of the bill.

The same pattern shows up in people in several other places, and it is one of the more interesting threads in this whole area: a great deal of skilled performance turns out to involve getting the part of you that interprets to be quiet long enough for the part that perceives to do its job.

What the whole thing is actually telling us

Put the pieces together and you get something more interesting than a chimpanzee beating people at a game.

Start with what the practice studies established: this ability is trainable, and practice closes most of the human-chimp gap. So it was never really a story about better equipment. That leaves a much better question. What was each mind shaped and practised for?

Ayumu's answer is a chimpanzee's life, where taking in a fast-vanishing scene at a glance earns its keep. Your answer is symbols. That is the thing our species went all in on, and it is why you can read this sentence, hold a conversation about something that has not happened yet, and carry an idea from your head into somebody else's. Ai's lower score is the receipt: the symbolic layer is not free, and on this particular task it charges rent.

Which puts the usual way of ranking minds in a strange light. The chimp beats you at the part of a standard intelligence test that most resembles this game, the fast-uptake part, the bit that asks how much you can take in and juggle in a moment. He does not come close on the symbolic side, and no amount of training takes him there. Two very different kinds of capable, each built for a different life, and no single ladder with one of them above the other.

How this game plays fair

The scored round is Ayumu's real timed setting, five numbers at 210 milliseconds, because that is the one with a published chimpanzee score and a published human score to sit beside it. Every single flash is timed against your screen's own refresh rather than simply assumed to be right, and any try where the timing wandered or the screen skipped a frame gets thrown out and replaced instead of quietly counted. You can see the whole log on your results.

His nine-number feat gets its own game, built the way he actually did it: every number visible until you touch the 1, and your own commit time as the score to beat. Two tasks, two honest comparisons, rather than one impressive-sounding condition that nobody has ever been measured on.

Then you get the dials, and the practice Ayumu got and you did not. Your map fills in, your edge moves, and that moving edge is the actual point of all of this.
