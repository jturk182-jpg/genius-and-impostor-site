The story behind the game

Meet Ayumu

A young chimpanzee in a research lab in Japan can do something most humans can't. Numbers flash on a screen, vanish, and he taps where they were, in order, from memory. He has been doing this since he was five years old.

You've experienced firsthand just how good he is. Now here is what it means.

When this study came out, the headlines were about a chimp being more intelligent than humans.

A chimpanzee, beating university students at a memory test. Exactly the kind of thing that shows up on an IQ test.

And if you believe that IQ tests measure intelligence, then the conclusion is hard to avoid. We humans may not be the smartest primates after all.

Then again, maybe instead it reveals something fundamentally broken about what we think intelligence is. And maybe the very idea of being able to rank different minds, including those of different species, is fundamentally flawed.

Here is what is actually happening when you play this game.

The numbers flash for 210 milliseconds. It's too short for you to scan across the screen. You have to get it all from a single glance. One mental snapshot.

Ayumu takes that snapshot and sees it for what it is. You do not. And the reason is not that his memory is bigger or his eyes are faster. It is that your brain adds a step.

When Ayumu sees a 7 he sees a shape. When you see a 7 you see a symbol, and your mind turns that shape into a concept: the number seven.

A concept with meaning and associations and a place in a sequence. That conversion happens instantly, and you cannot stop it. By the time the number reaches you, it has stopped being a shape in a location and become an idea. That extra step takes time, and this game punishes time.

In fact, there was an older chimpanzee in the same lab who had spent years learning to use symbols. Ai wasn't nearly as good as Ayumu. She'd built more of the symbolic machinery, and that extra step appeared to cost her on this task, too.

But, of course, we humans get something amazing in return for that cost.

The thing that slows you down on this game is the same thing that lets you read this sentence. Plan something for next week. Explain an idea to someone who is not in the room. Hold a conversation about things that have not happened yet. Move a thought from inside your head into someone else's.

That is what a human brain is built for. Not because it is a better brain, but because that is what human life demands.

Your brain is built for language, abstraction, meaning. Every waking moment, your mind is converting raw experience into symbols you can store, combine, and share. It is the thing that makes everything distinctly human possible.

Ayumu's brain is built for his life. Fast spatial capture, taking in a whole scene at a glance, earns its keep in the world a chimpanzee navigates. He is extraordinary at what he needs to be extraordinary at. So are you. Every species is good at solving the problems it needs to solve.

Which means those headlines had it wrong.

Because those headlines were based on the idea that who is the smarter species was a question that even made sense. They were based on the idea that there was some general quantity of "smarts" in every brain.

Turns out that's a dumb idea. Because "smarter" does not mean anything without asking at what and for what life.

There is no single scale with one species above the other. There is no general quality called intelligence that one creature has more of. There are brains, built by the demands of the lives they serve, each remarkable at what those demands require.

The game did not show who is smarter. It showed that the ranking does not exist.

And you did not lose because something is wrong with your brain. You lost because your brain was busy doing the most important thing it does.
