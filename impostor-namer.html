Why a name works

When the worried voice is “you,” every thought it hands you arrives stamped as true, because it's coming from the same place all your real thoughts come from. Give it a name and you pull it a step away from yourself, close enough to hear, far enough to study. Psychologists have a term for creating that little bit of distance from a thought: cognitive defusion. It's the same move behind talking to yourself in the third person before a big moment, which turns out to steady people more than a pep talk does.

The sillier the name, the better it works, and that's not a gimmick. A voice called “you” carries the full weight of your identity. A voice called Professor Doom-Muffin carries the weight of a joke, and it is hard to be terrified of a joke. You're not trying to win an argument with it. You're just trying to remember, in the moment, that it's a character, not the truth.

This voice is one of the two you'll meet in The Genius and the Impostor. The Impostor is the nervous narrator that gets loud when the stakes go up and talks over the part of you that actually knows how to do the thing. Naming it is the first, smallest way to turn its volume down. The book is where that whole story goes, and where the turning-down gets real.
