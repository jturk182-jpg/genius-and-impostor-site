The full story

Seven never changes, and it never needs to.

The bottleneck

The memory you used to hold those letters is small. Most people can keep about seven separate things in it at once, and in careful measurements the number often comes out closer to four. Psychologists have known this since the 1950s, when George Miller called it the magical number seven.

That limit is real, it is narrow, and practice does not widen it. Every trick your brain has for getting more through it works the same way: it makes each thing bigger. Miller called those bigger things chunks. A word is a chunk of letters, and a sentence is a chunk of words. You built those chunks so long ago that you cannot remember doing it, which is why round 3 felt like nothing at all.

The runner who could hold 79 digits

Working memory gets measured in the lab with a digit span test: someone reads you a list of numbers and you say the list back. Most adults manage about seven. In 1980, the psychologist Anders Ericsson and his colleagues paid a college student, called S.F. in the paper, to practice this test for an hour a day. He started at seven like everyone else. After about 230 hours of practice, he could hear 79 digits once and repeat every one of them back.

Had he grown his memory? The researchers checked. They switched from digits to letters, and his span dropped straight back to six, exactly where it had started. His working memory had not changed at all. What he had built was a way to hear digits as something he already knew deeply. He ran cross-country, so a string like 3492 became “three minutes, forty-nine point two, close to a record mile,” and a list of 79 digits became a short list of race times. He had built bigger chunks for digits, and for nothing else. Your round 1 score is S.F.’s letter span. Practice never moved it for him, and it does not need to move for you either, because practice builds bigger chunks instead.

Ericsson, Chase & Faloon (1980). Acquisition of a memory skill. Science, 208, 1181–1182.

Chess masters and random boards

Chess gave the same result decades earlier. Researchers showed chess players a board from a real game for about five seconds and asked them to rebuild it from memory. Masters put nearly every piece back in place. Beginners managed four or five. Then the researchers scattered the same pieces around the board at random, and the masters’ advantage disappeared. They did no better than the beginners.

So the masters were never remembering pieces one at a time. They were seeing whole attacking patterns they had met thousands of times before, and one pattern takes up one slot. On a random board there are no patterns, so a master is back to counting pieces, which is exactly what you were doing with the letters in round 1.

Chase & Simon (1973). Perception in chess. Cognitive Psychology, 4, 55–81. Building on de Groot (1965).

It works this way in every field

A skilled musician hears a chord and knows its flavor without adding up the notes, and hears a familiar progression as one shape. A quarterback reads an entire defensive formation in a glance, where a rookie sees eleven separate players. A fluent speaker holds a whole sentence without noticing, which is why speaking your own language feels like nothing. In every case the working space stayed the same size, and years of exposure built bigger chunks to fill it.

This is also why drilling raw memory span on its own does so little for anything else. Practice does not widen the channel. It builds bigger chunks, and it builds them only in the material you practice. S.F. got his 79 digits from hundreds of hours with digits. A musician gets the chords from hundreds of hours with chords.

What a memory test is really measuring

A span test measures the width of the raw channel, which means it measures you before the learning: the state that practice makes irrelevant. Everything that made you capable in rounds 2 and 3, the years of building bigger chunks, is exactly the part such a test cannot see. So a number from a test like that describes the one part of the system that starts out about the same in everyone. Expertise is what happens when you no longer need to squeeze through it.

Where this story comes from

Everything in this game is one small piece of The Genius and the Impostor, a book about the two characters who share your head. The Genius is the vast non-conscious machinery that turned letters into words and words into meaning for you today, without ever asking for your attention. The Impostor is the conscious narrator, the part of you that was straining to hold seven letters in round 1 and keeping score the whole time.

The book has a name for what you just felt: the Effort Inversion. The work you can feel is a poor guide to where the skill lives. The book is about how the Genius gets built, and how to keep the Impostor out of its way when something real is on the line.

One more memory game

A chimpanzee named Ayumu beats nearly every human at a game where numbers flash on a screen and vanish, and for years that was taken as proof that chimps have better working memory than we do. Then someone checked how much practice each side had. Try his game and see where you land.
